Four words, and what they are really like.
Funnel
The steps people go through, and who drops out at each.
Think of it as: a wedding: invited, RSVP’d, turned up, queued for biryani, actually ate. Everyone drops somewhere.
Baseline
What normal looks like, to compare against.
Think of it as: the pitch report: 280 is a great score on one pitch and a collapse on another.
Cohort
A group that starts together, followed over time.
Think of it as: the 2019 batch: compare how they are doing five years on, not the whole college today.
Correlation vs cause
Moving together is not one causing the other.
Think of it as: the rooster crows before sunrise. The rooster does not cause the sunrise.

Game
Hypothesis or hunch?
The test: can you say what you would see in the data if it is true, and what would prove it wrong?
“Our customers are losing interest.”HunchSeen how? Nothing specific.
“If free delivery above ₹999 is working, more baskets will land just above ₹999 than before.”HypothesisSpecific. The basket data can prove it wrong.
“The new homepage is not working.”HunchNot working how, and measured by what?
“If the new search bar is confusing people, more searches will be followed by another search straight away.”HypothesisOne number settles it.
“Conversion will go up if we do more marketing.”HunchMore of what, how much, by when, compared with what?

Fix the hunch: “The new homepage is not working.”
Your turn.Rewrite it as a hypothesis in one sentence. What would you see if it were true?
One good version. If the homepage is the problem, the share of new visitors who click a product from it will fall after launch, while returning visitors, who mostly use search, stay flat.

A, B and C feel productive. Each one skips the same step.
A. Open the dashboard
Looking, but for what? With no question you find whatever confirms what you already suspected. You started with data instead of a problem.
B. Pull every breakdown
Five dimensions, dozens of cuts, two days of work. The decision is in seven hours. Most of those cuts won’t matter, and you can’t tell which ones yet.
C. Guess a hypothesis
Closest to good practice, but only if it’s falsifiable and comes before the data. Most hunches are formed while browsing, then renamed “hypothesis”.
D is not a trap. It is Lens 1. Understanding the problem well enough to know what to look at is the work, not a delay before it.
Day 1
The message you just read is a symptom. It isn’t the problem.
Analysts who respond to the symptom end up with a dashboard. Analysts who respond to the problem end up with a decision.
Symptom
“Conversion dropped 11%.”
An observation. True, but it doesn’t say what to look at or what to do.
Problem
“Something revenue-critical may be broken. We must decide today whether to treat it as an incident or as noise.”
A decision with stakes and a deadline. Until you can write this, you can’t act.
Day 1
How I used to approach a problem, and what I’d change
A good first draft. Four upgrades make it reliable.
  1. Get context; define the problem
  2. Understand what’s happening and the impact
  3. Think like the user; build hypotheses
  4. Read how the industry approaches it
  5. Define the right metrics
  6. Choose the analytical method
  7. Draw insights
  8. Build an intervention plan
  9. Tell the story
Check it’s real before explaining it
Tracking changes, definition changes and calendar effects all look like behaviour change. Rule them out first.
Ask where before why
Decompose the metric until the change lives in one branch. Hypotheses about a located problem are sharper.
Look outside for what changed, not only for best practice
Releases, bank outages, competitor sales, regulation. The release calendar is a dataset.
It’s a loop, not a line
Each hypothesis the data kills sends you back to step 3 with a sharper question.

The nine lenses on one page.
Frame it
1. Define the problem
Questions for people: what decision, by when, and what “done” looks like.
2. Understand what’s happening
Questions for the data: is it real, where is it, how big is it?
3. Think like the user

Investigate it
4. Look outside

5. Pick the right metrics
One primary, a few diagnostics, one or two guardrails.
6. Choose the method
The cheapest analysis that tells your hypotheses apart.
Act on it
7. Insights, not findings
Finding plus mechanism plus consequence. Would the reader act differently?
8. Intervention plan
Action, owner, sequence, risk, and what you will watch.

Is it real?

Where is it?

Conversion = Reach cart × Start checkout × Complete address × Pay successfully

1234Lens 5 of 9: Pick the right metrics6789
One primary metric. A few diagnostics. One or two guardrails.
The common failure isn’t choosing the wrong metric. It’s choosing too many and losing the signal.
Your choices, decoded. From your options: C is this lens. A buries the signal, and B and D are too blunt.
Primary
Tells you whether the decision worked. You can name it in one sentence, with its numerator and denominator.
Diagnostic
Explains why the primary moved. Each one maps to a hypothesis it can confirm or kill.
Guardrail
Catches you winning on the primary while losing somewhere else. A fix that breaks a guardrail can be worse than the problem.
Think of it as a cricket match: the win is the primary, the run rate is the diagnostic, and an injury is the guardrail.

Question typeAsksTypical methodsWatch out
DescriptiveWhat happened?Trends, funnels, breakdownsNever tells you why

The numbers, the law and the honest alternative.
Read the numbers
+9% conversion but only +6% revenue: revenue per order fell about 3%. Rushed shoppers bought cheaper. A one-week test can’t see regret, returns or lost trust.
Read the law
India’s Consumer Protection Authority issued dark-pattern guidelines in November 2023. “False urgency” is the first of 13 specified dark patterns.
Test the honest version
Show real low stock and real sale deadlines only. Measure it with 90-day repeat and return rate as guardrails.
The framework tells you whether an intervention works. It can’t tell you whether you should ship it. Raise it, quantify the risk, and put it in writing.
